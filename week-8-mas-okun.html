The big picture

This week answers two questions

1. What makes inflation go up or down? The answer is the MAS equation.

2. How is unemployment linked to how much the economy produces? The answer is Okun's law.

Put them together and you get a chain: more output → fewer people unemployed → higher inflation.

Step 1

Prices are wages plus a bit extra

A firm works out what its workers cost, then adds something on top to make a profit. That "something on top" is the mark-up (μ).

P = (1 + μ) × W

ExampleBaking a cake costs a bakery €10 in wages. It adds a 20% mark-up (μ = 0.2), so it charges €12.

The mark-up is smaller when there's lots of competition. If there are five bakeries on the street, none of them can charge much extra or customers go next door.

If wages go up, prices go up.
Step 2

What decides wages?

W = Pᵉ × (1 − αU + z)

Read it as three plain ideas:

  • Pᵉ: if workers expect prices to rise, they ask for more pay so they can still afford the same things.
  • −αU: if lots of people are unemployed, workers can't push for much, because someone else would take the job. Wages stay down.
  • +z: strong unions and minimum wage laws push wages up.
Expected prices and worker power push wages up. Unemployment holds them down.
Step 3: the main equation

The MAS equation in plain words

Put steps 1 and 2 together and turn "prices" into "how fast prices rise" (inflation). You get:

π = πᵉ + (μ + z) − αU

This year's inflation = what people expected + a push from business mark-ups and worker power − a cooling effect from unemployment.

Worked examplePeople expect 2% inflation (πᵉ = 2). Mark-up and worker power add 3 (μ + z = 3). Unemployment is 6% and α = 0.5, so unemployment takes away 0.5 × 6 = 3.
π = 2 + 3 − 3 = 2%. Inflation matches what people expected, so nothing changes. That makes 6% the NAIRU here.
Now unemployment falls to 4%Unemployment only takes away 0.5 × 4 = 2.
π = 2 + 3 − 2 = 3%. Lower unemployment means higher inflation.

You don't need to be able to derive this equation, only understand and use it. You can try it in the MAS lab tab.

Step 4

Why people's guesses matter so much

The first part of the equation, πᵉ, is just people's guess about inflation. Guesses matter because they change behaviour:

  • Workers who expect prices to rise ask for bigger pay rises.
  • Firms that expect costs to rise put their prices up early, and assume their rivals will too.

So expecting inflation helps cause inflation. If this keeps looping, it's called a wage-price spiral:

Prices rose→People expect more rises→Workers want more pay→Firms' costs go up→Firms raise prices→↻ repeat
Step 5

How people make their guesses

The notes use adaptive expectations: people look at what happened recently and adjust their guess a bit.

πᵉt = πᵉt−1 + λ(πt − πᵉt−1)

In words: new guess = old guess + λ × (how wrong the old guess was).

ExampleYou guessed 2% inflation last year, but it turned out to be 6%. You were 4 points too low.
If λ = 0.5, you add half your mistake: new guess = 2 + 0.5 × 4 = 4%.
If λ = 0, you ignore the mistake and still guess 2%.
If λ = 1, you add the whole mistake and now guess 6%.
  • Inflation inertia: once people expect high inflation, it keeps going even after the original cause has disappeared.
  • Credibility: if everyone trusts the central bank to hit its target, guesses stay at the target (they're "anchored"), and inflation stays low more easily.
Step 6

The minimum wage: two stories

Story A: cost-push (theory)Firms have to pay more, so they raise prices and produce less. SRAS shifts left. Prices go up and output goes down.
Story B: demand-pull (evidence)Lower-paid workers get more money and spend it. AD shifts right. Prices go up and output goes up.

Real-world evidence mostly supports Story B. The price effect is small, because better-paid workers are more productive and firms sell more.

Step 7

History in four lines

  • 1950s: people's guesses never changed (λ ≈ 0). Lower unemployment reliably meant a bit more inflation.
  • 1970s: guesses kept going up (λ ≈ 1). Inflation stayed high even when unemployment rose, and wage-price spirals broke out.
  • The fix: governments agreed limits on pay rises with unions, and central banks raised interest rates a lot.
  • 1990s onwards: central banks announced a 2% inflation target and explained their decisions openly. People trusted it, and inflation became low and steady.
Step 8

Okun's law: output and jobs

When the economy produces more, firms need more workers, so unemployment falls. When it produces less, firms let people go.

U − UF = −β(Y − YF)

In words: how far unemployment is from normal = −β × how far output is from potential. The minus sign just means they move in opposite directions.

ExampleNormal unemployment is 5% and β = 0.5. The economy is producing 2% below potential.
U − 5 = −0.5 × (−2) = +1, so unemployment is 6%.

The NAIRU (UF) is the "normal" unemployment rate when the economy is at potential. At that rate, inflation stays steady.

β is usually small. Output and unemployment don't move one-for-one, because:

  • Firms keep staff in bad times, expecting things to pick up (labour hoarding).
  • Firms cut hours instead of jobs.
  • Some people give up looking for work, so they stop counting as unemployed (discouraged workers).
  • Firms get more productive, so they need fewer new hires.
  • The jobs being created need different skills from the people looking for work (skill mismatch).
  • Welfare payments can make some people less keen to take a job.

Okun's law is something economists observed in the data, not a theory, and it's mainly about the short to medium term.

Wrap-up

The whole week in four sentences

  • Inflation = expected inflation + business and worker power − the cooling effect of unemployment (the MAS).
  • People's guesses about inflation help cause inflation, so a trusted central bank matters.
  • More output means less unemployment, but not one-for-one (Okun's law).
  • Chain them together: more output → less unemployment → more inflation. That's why the SRAS slopes upward.

Week Eight in brief

The notes build one equation linking inflation to unemployment (the MAS), then one linking unemployment to GDP (Okun's law). Together they complete the SRAS story.

1 · Where the MAS comes from

Wage and price setting

  • Workers want the highest wage their skills allow. Firms want to pay as little as possible while still getting the skills they need.
  • Prices are a mark-up on wages: P = (1 + μ)W, where μ is the mark-up.
  • Wages depend on expected prices, unemployment and labour market institutions: W = Pᵉ(1 − αU + z).
  • Substitute, convert from price levels to inflation rates, and you get the Modified Aggregate Supply (MAS) equation. You don't need to reproduce the derivation, but you do need to understand and apply the result.

2a · Expected inflation (πᵉ)

Expectations feed into today's inflation

  • Wage setting: workers who've seen high inflation expect more, so they demand higher nominal wages to protect their purchasing power. Unit labour costs rise and firms raise prices.
  • Price setting: firms build expected cost rises into today's prices. They're confident rivals will do the same, and they'll pay menu costs rather than fall behind.
  • This can become a wage-price spiral. These were common in the 1970s and are rarer now because central banks operate differently.
Past inflation→Higher expectations→Higher wage demands→Higher costs→Higher prices→↻ back to expectations
2b · How expectations form

Adaptive expectations

πet = πet−1 + λ(πt − πet−1)

  • People update last year's forecast by a fraction λ (between 0 and 1) of their forecast error.
  • λ = 0: expectations never move (the 1950s). λ close to 1: people expect last year's inflation again (the 1970s onwards).
  • Why it matters: inflation inertia (inflation persists after its cause is gone), the monetary policy challenge (unanchored expectations are costly to break) and central bank credibility (a believed target anchors expectations).
  • Rational expectations use all available information, but past inflation is still an important part of it.
2c · Mark-up and institutions (μ + z)

Competition, institutions and the minimum wage

  • More competition means a lower mark-up μ. Firms chase volume rather than higher prices, so the price level is lower.
  • z covers labour market structures (bargaining, minimum wage laws) that keep wages, and therefore prices, higher.
  • Theory: a higher minimum wage raises firms' costs. SRAS shifts left (A → B): cost-push inflation, with higher prices and lower output.
  • Evidence: the cost-push effect is very small. Higher pay raises productivity, and poorer households spend the extra income, so AD shifts right: demand-pull inflation, with higher prices and higher output.

Cost-push: SRAS shifts left, A → B

Demand-pull: AD shifts right, A → B

2d · Current unemployment (−αU)

Higher unemployment, lower inflation

  • GDP falls → unemployment rises → AD falls → firms are left with unsold stock → they cut prices.
  • α measures how much inflation changes when unemployment changes.
  • The link can fail: prices are sticky, and high expectations can keep inflation up even while unemployment rises.
History

From stable to broken and back

  • 1950s: a stable inflation–unemployment trade-off. Expectations were constant (λ ≈ 0).
  • 1970s: λ ≈ 1. Rising expectations kept inflation high even as unemployment rose. The relationship broke down and wage-price spirals and strikes followed.
  • The fix: social contracts capped wage rises, and central banks raised interest rates sharply.
  • 1990s on: inflation targeting, eventually 2%, with more transparent central banks. Inflation became low and stable, and the old relationship reappeared.
3 · Unemployment and GDP

Okun's law (Arthur Okun, 1962)

Ut − UF = −β(Yt − YF)

  • An observed (empirical, not theoretical) inverse link: output above potential means unemployment below its natural rate. In this version, causality runs from Y to U.
  • UF is unemployment at potential GDP: the natural rate, or NAIRU (non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment), where inflation is stable.
  • β (Okun's coefficient) is usually small, well below one-for-one. The reasons: social welfare, labour hoarding, cutting hours instead of jobs, discouraged workers leaving the labour force, productivity gains and skill mismatches.
  • It's a short-to-medium-term, cyclical relationship. It doesn't hold for long-run structural change, and it varies across economies and time.

Check yourself: why can inflation rise even when unemployment is rising?

If expected inflation rises faster than the −αU term falls, actual inflation still goes up. That's the 1970s story: unemployment rose, but expectations rose more, so the old trade-off broke down.

Check yourself: what's special about the NAIRU point on the chart?

At the NAIRU, actual inflation equals expected inflation, so expectations don't need revising and inflation is stable. Below the NAIRU, inflation beats expectations and they get revised up. Above it, the reverse happens.
